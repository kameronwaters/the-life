# fish — the "fishy" Bible stories

Every passage that gets thrown at a plant-based reading of Jesus because a fish is in it.
One line per story: where it is, what it actually says, and the honest verdict.
Deeper work lives in its own file.

| Story | Text | What the text actually does | Verdict for the vegan reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jesus' beach breakfast | John 21:1-14 | Charcoal fire with `opsarion` (relish) and bread; Jesus tells them to bring their catch; he distributes bread "and did the same with the fish". Never says Jesus ate. | Majority of scholars: ch. 21 is a later epilogue. Strong literary case; the "end of 2nd century" dating is not supportable. See [john-21-fish-fry-fact-check.md](./john-21-fish-fry-fact-check.md). |
| Miraculous catch at the call | Luke 5:1-11 | Nets fill; Peter falls down; Jesus: "from now on you will catch men." They leave everything. Marcion's gospel had this scene (Tertullian, *Against Marcion* 4.9). | The point of the story is leaving the nets. Fishing is what they quit. |
| Fishers of men | Mark 1:16-20; Matt 4:18-22 | Call by the lake; "I will make you fishers of men." Absent from John 1. | Metaphor for recruiting people. Says nothing about eating fish. |
| Loaves and fishes (5,000) | Mark 6:30-44; Matt 14:13-21; Luke 9:10-17; John 6:1-15 | Five loaves, two `opsaria` (John) / `ichthyes` (Synoptics). Multiplied and distributed. | The fish is in every version. Matt 16:9-10 and Mark 8:19-20 recall only the loaves, which suggests the bread was the remembered core. Inference, not proof. |
| Loaves and fishes (4,000) | Mark 8:1-10; Matt 15:32-39 | Seven loaves, "a few small fish." | Same as above. |
| Parable of the net | Matt 13:47-50 | Kingdom like a net gathering every kind; good kept, bad thrown out. | Pure metaphor for judgment. |
| Coin in the fish's mouth | Matt 17:24-27 | Peter told to cast a hook; first fish has a stater for the temple tax. | Only in Matthew; no parallel. Jesus does not eat it. |
| Fish, not a serpent | Matt 7:9-10; Luke 11:11-12 | A father gives bread and fish, not a stone or snake. | Everyday food illustration; describes the culture, not a command. |
| Broiled fish after resurrection | Luke 24:41-43 | "They gave him a piece of broiled fish, and he took it and ate it in their presence." | The single verse where Jesus is said to eat flesh. Some manuscripts add "and honeycomb". Luke 24:35 says they knew him "in the breaking of the bread." The hardest text; do not pretend otherwise. |
| Children, have you any food? | John 21:5 | Greek `prosphagion`, "something eaten with bread." | Word choice, not a verdict. See fact-check. |
| 153 fish | John 21:11 | Large fish, net not torn. | Augustine: triangle of 17. Bauckham: Ezekiel 47:10, En-gedi (17) and En-eglaim (153). Symbolic number either way. |
| Jonah | Jonah 1-2; Matt 12:40 | Swallowed by a "great fish"; sign of three days. | Fish as instrument of God, not food. |
| Tobit's fish | Tobit 6:1-9 | Fish attacks Tobias; heart, liver, gall used for healing and exorcism. | Deuterocanonical. Fish as medicine. |
| Ezekiel's river of fish | Ezekiel 47:1-12 | Living water from the temple heals the Dead Sea; fishermen from En-gedi to En-eglaim. | Restoration imagery behind John 21. |
| Leviathan / sea creatures | Gen 1:20-22; Ps 104:25-26; Job 41 | Creation of sea life, God plays with Leviathan. | Sea creatures as God's delight, not stock. |

## greek words to know

- `ichthys` — a fish, the animal. John 21:6, 8, 11.
- `opsarion` — diminutive of `opson`, "cooked relish eaten with bread"; in practice often small fish. John 6:9, 6:11, 21:9, 21:10, 21:13. Only John uses it.
- `prosphagion` — "something to eat with bread." John 21:5 only.
- `ichthydion` — "little fish." Mark 8:7; Matt 15:34.

## the honest state of play

The vegan case does not need John 21 to be fake. It needs three things, all textually defensible:
Jesus called fishermen away from their nets; the one feeding miracle both halves of the tradition
remember is bread; and the risen Jesus gives food, he is only once said to eat it (Luke 24:43).
Everything beyond that is argument about the history of the text, and that argument has to be made
carefully or it discredits the rest.
